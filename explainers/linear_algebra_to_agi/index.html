A guided intuition-builder

The cat sat on the ___

That's it. That's the whole example. A five-word sentence with a gap at the end, the kind you'd give a child, or a very small machine.

By the end of this page, you'll watch seventy years of mathematics compete to fill that blank — from a hand-counted probability table to a neural network deciding, on its own initiative, to check the weather and text you about it. Same sentence, every stage. Watch what changes, and notice why each change was necessary, not just fashionable.

Act 1 · Foundations

Before anything can learn, it has to count

A computer can't read "cat." It can only hold numbers. So the very first move — the one everything else is built on — is to turn words into vectors: lists of numbers that place each word somewhere in a space, positioned so that words used in similar ways end up near each other.

Give cat, dog, mat, grass, and floor two coordinates each — how "outdoor" a word feels, and how strongly it associates with an animal sitting on it — and a crude picture of meaning falls out of pure geometry.

Toy coordinates, hand-placed for intuition — not learned from data (yet).

Geometry gives us similarity. But to actually fill in the blank we want a probability: given everything we've seen, how likely is each ending? The oldest clean answer to that question is Bayes' theorem — flip a conditional probability around using only things you can actually count.

Counting works when the world is six sentences long. It collapses the moment context gets rich — you can't count your way through infinite sentences. We need something that generalizes.

Act 2 · Classical ML

Stop counting. Start fitting a line.

Instead of a lookup table, describe each candidate ending with numeric features — say, indoor-ness and surface-softness — and let a model learn weights that combine those features into a score. This is the same "mat vs. grass" question, but now it's a line drawn through feature space.

How does the model find that line? It needs a way to measure "how wrong am I right now" — a loss function — and a way to shrink that wrongness step by step.

A single line can only bend the world in one way. Real language needs curves, combinations, exceptions-to-exceptions. One line isn't enough — but a network of them might be.

The trick is stacking many of them. Feed our two toy features — indoor-ness, surface-softness — into a small hidden layer, and let each hidden neuron learn its own bent little rule. The output layer then votes across those rules.

But how does the network learn which weights to use? The same gradient descent as before — just applied one layer at a time, using the chain rule to trace blame backward from the output to every weight that contributed to it. That backward pass is backpropagation.

A three-node hidden layer can separate mat from grass. It cannot read a paragraph, recognize a face, or understand grammar. For that, we need more layers — a lot more — and a reason to believe more layers actually help.

Act 4 · Deep Learning at Scale

Stack it deeper. Now keep it trainable.

Stacking layers sounds like a free lunch — more layers, more expressive power. In practice, early deep networks fell apart: gradients passed back through many layers of sigmoid neurons would shrink at each step, until the earliest layers received a signal too faint to learn from. The vanishing gradient problem stalled deep networks for years.

Two unglamorous fixes mattered more than any single clever architecture: the ReLU activation (f(z) = max(0, z)), which doesn't squash its gradient toward zero the way sigmoid does, and enough compute and data — GPUs originally built for video games turned out to be extraordinarily good at the matrix multiplications neural networks are made of.

Gradient magnitude traveling backward through 8 stacked layers.

With those fixes, scale started to pay off in ways that felt qualitatively new. Convolutional networks learned to see. Recurrent networks and their gated successor, the LSTM, learned to hold a thread of context across a sequence — the first real attempt at machines "remembering" the start of a sentence by the time they reached the end of it.

Sources & dates
  • 1989 Yann LeCun applies backpropagation to convolutional networks for handwritten digit recognition.
  • 1997 Sepp Hochreiter & Jürgen Schmidhuber publish the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) architecture.
  • 1997 IBM's Deep Blue defeats Garry Kasparov — a search-and-heuristics triumph, not a learning one, but a milestone in the public "can machines think" narrative.
  • 2006 Geoffrey Hinton's work on deep belief networks helps re-brand and revive multilayer learning as "deep learning."
  • 2012 Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton's AlexNet wins ImageNet by a huge margin, trained on GPUs — the moment deep learning became impossible to ignore.

LSTMs process a sentence one word at a time, in order — which means word five has to wait for words one through four before it even starts, and by the time it arrives, the memory of word one has already begun to fade. That sequential bottleneck is about to break.

Act 5 · The Transformer

Stop reading in order. Look at everything at once.

Instead of marching through "The cat sat on the" one token at a time, let every token look directly at every other token, and decide for itself which ones matter. That's self-attention: each word forms a query ("what am I looking for?"), compares it against every word's key ("what do I offer?"), and blends in their values in proportion to the match.

Each token asks a question (Q), compares it against every token's label (K), and blends their content (V) in proportion to the match.

\text{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
Q

K

V

dₖ

Predicting the blank: "cat" and "sat" carry the most weight — subject and verb constrain the object most.

Because every token can attend to every other token simultaneously, there's no sequential bottleneck — the whole sentence is processed in parallel, on hardware built exactly for that kind of parallel matrix math. That single property is why transformers scaled where RNNs stalled.

  • 2015 He et al., ResNet — residual connections make networks hundreds of layers deep trainable.
  • June 2017 Vaswani et al., "Attention Is All You Need" (arXiv:1706.03762, NeurIPS 2017) — the Transformer.

A transformer that can weigh context is still just a scoring function. Turn it around to predict one token at a time, train it on a large enough slice of the internet, and something else starts to happen.

Nothing here is qualitatively new — it's the same attention, the same softmax, the same gradient descent from three acts ago. What changed was scale: parameter counts, training tokens, and compute all grew by orders of magnitude, and capabilities that weren't explicitly trained for — arithmetic, translation, rudimentary reasoning — began to emerge as side effects of getting very good at next-token prediction. Instruction tuning and RLHF then shaped that raw prediction engine into something that follows requests rather than just completing text.

A model that predicts text well can also predict which action comes next — including the action of using a tool, reading its result, and deciding what to do with that result. Wrap the prediction loop around itself, and it starts to act.

Act 7 · Agentic Systems

The same model, wrapped in a loop

Give the model a goal instead of a sentence to finish — "look up the weather and text me if it's raining" — plus a set of tools it's allowed to call and a scratchpad of memory, and let it run its own prediction loop repeatedly: think, act, observe, repeat, until the goal is satisfied.


text if raining 2call get_weather() 3observe: "rain, 68°F" 4decide: raining → notify 5call send_text()

Same next-token predictor from Act 6 — now the "tokens" it predicts are tool calls.

Multi-agent systems push this further: one model plans, another critiques, another executes, passing partial results back and forth — trading the reliability of a single long chain of thought for the redundancy of several shorter, checked ones. None of this required new mathematics. It required trusting the prediction loop enough to let it run unsupervised for more than one step.

Sources & dates
  • 2023 Function calling / tool use becomes a standard capability across major model APIs.
  • 2023 Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) becomes a common pattern for grounding model outputs in external data.
  • 2023–2024 Early autonomous agent frameworks and agentic coding tools proliferate; multi-agent orchestration patterns emerge.

A model that plans, calls tools, and checks its own work is doing something that looks a great deal like reasoning. The honest question left is whether it's doing the same kind of thing a mind does — and what, if anything, is still missing.

Act 8 · Toward AGI?

What's still missing — three honest guesses

Nobody agrees on how much further this path — bigger transformers, more data, tool use — actually goes toward general intelligence. Three positions, each held by serious researchers, are worth holding in tension rather than resolving.

Scale is (mostly) enough

Capabilities have emerged reliably from scaling parameters, data, and compute so far, with no clean wall in sight. Richard Sutton's "Bitter Lesson" argues that general methods leveraging computation consistently beat hand-engineered ones. Kaplan et al.'s scaling laws gave this position a quantitative backbone.

Scale plus new architecture

Next-token prediction over text may not be enough to build an internal model of how the physical world works. Yann LeCun has argued for world models and architectures like JEPA that learn by predicting in an abstract representation space rather than in token space.

Neural-symbolic hybrid

Pure neural networks are pattern matchers at heart, and struggle with systematic, compositional reasoning. Gary Marcus and others argue that robust reasoning needs some marriage of learned perception with explicit symbolic structure — not one paradigm winning outright.

Other candidate ingredients get raised often enough to name: continual learning (updating beliefs after deployment instead of freezing at a training cutoff), inference-time search and reasoning (spending more computation per problem rather than more parameters), and embodiment (the argument, associated with roboticists like Rodney Brooks, that some kinds of understanding may only arise from acting in a physical world).

Return, one last time, to "The cat sat on the ___." A hand-counted table got it right seventy-some years before anyone had heard the word "transformer." What changed across every act of this page was never the goal — fill in the blank, sensibly — but the machinery's capacity to generalize past the examples it was shown, and, in the last two acts, to act on its own conclusions. Whether that trajectory bends into something we'd call a mind is, honestly, still an open question. Anyone who tells you they know the timeline is selling something.
